New codecheckers usually have an onboarding call with a [CODECHECK editor](/guide/community-workflow-editor) before their first check.
This page lists the topics of that call, so you can prepare for it and look things up afterwards.
It complements, but does not replace, the [workflow for codecheckers](/guide/community-workflow-codechecker).

We like to compare a CODECHECK to a craft: you learn it by doing it, with a mentor at your side, more than by reading about it.
So don't worry if not everything below makes sense right away - it will after your first check, and you can always ask.

## Onboarding topics

### Getting to know each other

- When and why did you register as a codechecker?
- What is your background - are you a researcher, a developer, a research software engineer, a student, or a data steward?
- Have you already been involved in a CODECHECK, as an author or as a codechecker?
  Your earlier certificates might already be in the [register](/register).
  If you have done reproducibility checks that are not in the register yet, let us know - we may be able to add them.
{:.full-width}

### Roles

A CODECHECK involves three roles:

- the **author** provides the workflow, i.e., code, data, and instructions ([author guide](/guide/community-workflow-author));
- the **codechecker** runs the workflow following the author's instructions and writes the certificate ([codechecker guide](/guide/community-workflow-codechecker));
- the **CODECHECK editor** assigns codecheckers, mentors them, and looks after the publication of the certificate and its integration into the register ([editor guide](/guide/community-workflow-editor)).
{:.full-width}

### What a CODECHECK is - and what it is not

A CODECHECK tests whether the _instructions_ provided by the authors lead to the _results_ the authors report.
It does not assess whether the code is good or correct, i.e., we do not review code quality, naming, or modularisation.
The code can be a total mess - if the mess comes with a `Makefile` or a README that lets you run it with a couple of commands, that is good enough for a CODECHECK.
For the same reason, it doesn't matter much if large parts of the code were generated by AI.

The results do not have to match to the last pixel.
Figures can look slightly different because of a different operating system, screen, or graphics driver.
What matters is that the figures show the same data, and that you record the differences you notice.

Keep the [CODECHECK principles](/project/#the-codecheck-principles) in mind, especially the first one: **codecheckers record but do not fix**.
You capture the current state of the workflow and report problems back to the authors.

### The `codecheck.yml` file

The [CODECHECK configuration file](/spec/config/latest) captures the metadata of a check in one place, especially identifiers, so that we can connect the certificate, the paper, the authors, and the codecheckers.
Ideally, the authors create a first version of the file with their information.
In practice, most of the time the codechecker creates it.
AI coding assistants do this job very well: point the assistant to the specification and the preprint, and let it fill in the metadata, including the tedious lookup of the authors' ORCIDs.
Check the result and validate the file with `codecheck::validate_codecheck_yml_rules("codecheck.yml")` from the [`codecheck` R package](https://github.com/codecheckers/codecheck).

### Time horizons

There is no fixed time budget for a CODECHECK, but these numbers help to set expectations:

- **The first email to the authors** often goes out 10-20 minutes after you start, of which you actively work maybe two minutes:
  you download the code, read the README, type in the first commands, and come back to an error message about a missing dependency or a hard-coded path.
  Send that back to the authors right away.
- **Active time** is similar to a peer review, around two to three hours.
- **Computation time** should fit into a working day.
  A run overnight is fine, too, if you tested the workflow with a smaller configuration before.
  If the workflow needs much longer, or a GPU or an HPC cluster, ask the authors for a subset of the data, a configuration that runs on a CPU, and the matching reference results.
  Recreating the results for the subset is a successful CODECHECK, as long as the certificate summary states this limitation.
- **The gray area of fixing things**: if a single hard-coded path stands between you and the next step, fixing it yourself is faster than writing an email - go ahead and record the change.
  If there are twenty of them, it is the authors' job, not yours.
{:.full-width}

See also the [prerequisites](/guide/community-workflow-codechecker#prerequisites) in the codechecker guide.

### Communicating with the authors

You can talk directly to the authors, and you should do so early and often.
Email is the lowest bar and works for everyone.
Some authors are happy to work in GitHub issues or pull requests, and you can link to those in the certificate, but we don't require it.
We want to impose as little technology on the authors as possible:
a researcher who submits a few Python scripts and a CSV file as supplemental material, and has never used Git, is just as welcome as a fully containerised workflow with archived code and data.

### What goes into the certificate

The certificate documents _who_ checked _what_ and _how_.
Think of it as a reminder for your future self: what would you need to re-run the check in two years?
You don't need to document every step - references to the README or the authors' instructions are enough, and you can name the commands you ran.
Write down the things that made you think, tinker, or search.

A typical certificate has:

- a **summary** of two or three sentences, e.g., "The workflow was well prepared, I could run all commands with only minor warnings, and the results look the same.";
- the **outcomes**, i.e., the files from the manifest you recreated; if you want to do an excellent job, show the original and the reproduced figures side by side;
- **recommendations** for the authors, e.g., to state the expected execution time of each step in the README - authors know it by heart, codecheckers need to know it beforehand.
{:.full-width}

Keep a **feedback list** and update it, don't delete from it:
when the authors fix an item, strike it through and add "fixed by the authors".
This short story of questions and fixes shows the positive impact of the check.

There are [templates](/guide/community-workflow-codechecker#codechecker-tasks---extended-version) for word processors, R Markdown, and Jupyter, but you don't have to use them.
For inspiration, look at the [example certificates](/guide/community-workflow-overview#examples) and at the [good practices and common pitfalls](/guide/community-workflow-author#learning-from-examples) we have collected from them.

### Sharing the draft with the authors

Share the draft of the certificate with the authors before you publish it.
Authors often take up the feedback right away - out of ten items, nine may be fixed the next day.
This means you will have to update the certificate before publication, which is some extra work.
But it is rewarding work: the original research got better because you checked it.

### Publication

The certificate is published with a DOI, usually in the [CODECHECK community on Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/communities/codecheck/); the [codechecker guide](/guide/community-workflow-codechecker#codechecker-tasks---extended-version) describes the metadata to fill in.
We recommend to submit the draft record for review to the community, so that a CODECHECK editor can give feedback before the DOI is final.

The `codecheck.yml` file must be published somewhere, too.
Most often, it lives in a fork of the authors' repository in the [codecheckers organisation on GitHub](https://github.com/codecheckers/) or the [cdchck group on GitLab](https://gitlab.com/cdchck).
The fork is a snapshot under our control, because original repositories can be renamed or removed.
If the code is not on GitHub or GitLab, the file can also be published together with the certificate, e.g., on Zenodo.

### After publication: what the CODECHECK editor does

For your information - there is nothing you need to do here.
The CODECHECK editor adds the certificate to the register, which at its core is a table of certificate identifiers and where their `codecheck.yml` file is published.
From that table, scripts generate the [register website](/register), including a landing page for each certificate with a preview of the certificate and the abstract of the checked paper, and export the information to Wikidata.
See the [editor guide](/guide/community-workflow-editor) for the details.

### AI in codechecking

How to handle AI in CODECHECKs is work in progress, and we are gathering experience with it ourselves.
So far, we have learned the following:

- **AI is a big help with metadata**, e.g., to fill in the `codecheck.yml` file or to verify that all ORCIDs are correct.
- **Coding agents tend to run off and fix things.**
  Asked to look at a workflow, an agent may start writing a Dockerfile and refactoring the code without being told to.
  Give the agent the right context, in particular the CODECHECK principles: we investigate, we don't fix.
- **Don't accept any change that you don't understand.**
  A human has to verify every change, and a human codechecker signs off on the certificate.
- **A well-prepared workflow is easier to check - for humans and for AI.**
  Authors can use AI coding assistants to prepare their workflows, see [using AI coding assistants](/guide/community-workflow-author#using-ai-coding-assistants) in the author guide.

## Relevant for institutional efforts

Some institutions offer CODECHECKs as a service for their researchers, see the [institutional workflows](/institutions/).
If you are going to conduct checks as part of such a service, the following topics are relevant, too.

**There is no strict process.**
An institutional check can start in many ways, e.g., for a paper that is about to be submitted, for work in progress, or as part of a competition.
Define a workflow that works for you and for the research you check.
You don't need to write it down right away; after the first few checks you will know which steps are worth documenting, and we are happy to give feedback.

**Flexibility, but with transparency.**
Within an institution, authors may share data privately, sit next to you, or let you work on their computer because the data cannot leave it.
You may also use institutional resources, such as an HPC cluster or special hardware, and you might go further than a volunteer codechecker, e.g., by actively helping the authors to fix their workflow.
All of this is fine, as long as the certificate makes the setting transparent: state that the check was conducted as part of your institution's service, which resources you used, and where the codechecker was not independent of the authors.
Readers of the certificate can then judge for themselves.

**Your own template and name.**
Institutions may use their own certificate template, put their own logo up front, and call the certificate differently, e.g., "reproducibility report" - about half of the certificates in the register come from the reproducibility reviews of the AGILE conference and carry that name.
We care about the content.

**Where to publish.**
The register's automation, e.g., the preview images and the metadata on the landing pages, works best for certificates published on Zenodo.
If your institution publishes certificates in a different repository, talk to the CODECHECK editor early, so that we can extend the register for it.

**Register issues are public.**
The register issue for a check normally names the authors and links to their code or data.
Talk to the authors before you open an issue, and until then, keep in touch with the CODECHECK editor by email.
Over time, institutions can run the process themselves, i.e., open the register issue, reserve a certificate identifier, and submit the certificate to the Zenodo community, where the CODECHECK editor only reviews and accepts it.

**A separate list of codecheckers.**
Institutional codecheckers are listed separately from the volunteers in the [codecheckers repository](https://github.com/codecheckers/codecheckers#institutional-codecheckers), with a mapping of GitHub handle, ORCID, and institution so that we can connect the certificates correctly.
We don't contact institutional codecheckers with requests for volunteer checks.
You are of course welcome to also sign up as a volunteer, and then be listed twice.

**Your institution in the register.**
The register lists the certificates of each institution on its own page under [venues](/register/venues/).
Once the first certificates are published, let the CODECHECK editor know how your institution and its collection of certificates should be named.
